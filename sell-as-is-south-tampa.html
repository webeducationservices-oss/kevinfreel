For As-Is & Teardown Sellers

Selling a Teardown or As-Is Home in South Tampa

If your house is older, tired, or hard to insure, you have more options than the postcards in your mailbox suggest. Here is how these sales actually work, what moves your number up, and what quietly drags it down.

    The Basics

    What "As-Is" Actually Means Here

    Selling as-is means you are not making repairs and not handing out repair credits. That is the whole of it. It does not mean the buyer skips an inspection, and it does not mean you stop disclosing.

    Florida still requires you to disclose known material defects that are not readily observable. As-is changes who your buyer is. It does not change what you have to tell them. Anyone who suggests otherwise is setting you up for a problem after closing.

    The real decision is not whether to sell as-is. It is which of three very different buyers you are selling to, because they value your property in three completely different ways.

    Know Your Buyer

    Three Buyers, Three Very Different Numbers

    The same house can be worth noticeably different amounts depending on who is buying it and what they plan to do next.

    1

    The Builder

    Buys the lot, takes the house down, and builds new. Prices off what a finished new home sells for on your street, minus build cost and profit. When the lot supports what they want to build, this is usually the strongest number available to an as-is seller in South Tampa.

    2

    The Flipper

    Buys, renovates, resells. Needs resale margin, so local cash investors generally land around 80 to 88 percent of market value. Fast and certain, and a legitimate answer when the house is worth saving but you are not the one to save it.

    3

    The Retail Buyer

    A family who will live there. Pays the most on paper, but needs a mortgage, and a mortgage needs insurance. On an older home that is exactly where these deals fall apart, which is the part most sellers never see coming.

    The Part Nobody Warns You About

    Insurance Decides More Than You Think

    If you own an older South Tampa home, insurance may already be making this decision for you.

    • Florida carriers require a four point inspection on homes 20 years and older, covering roof, electrical, plumbing and HVAC.
    • Shingle roofs over 20 years are broadly uninsurable. Roofs in the 15 to 20 year range commonly fail a four point even when nothing is leaking.
    • Knob and tube wiring, common before the 1950s, is a hard stop for most standard carriers.
    • A failed four point can mean declined coverage, repairs required before binding, or non-renewal.

    Here is why that matters more than any kitchen upgrade: a house a buyer cannot insure is a house they usually cannot finance. When coverage gets hard, your retail buyer pool shrinks to people paying cash, and cash buyers expect a discount for the privilege.

    So the honest question is not "should I fix it up." It is "can I fix the things that are actually blocking a buyer, and would that spend come back to me?" Sometimes a roof pays for itself. Often, on a house a builder is going to take down anyway, it does not.

    Your Number

    What Moves It Up, What Drags It Down

    Raises your number

    • Lot dimensions that work. Width and depth decide what can be built, and that decides what a builder can pay. This is the single biggest lever and you cannot change it.
    • A street where new homes are already selling well. Builders underwrite the finished product, so recent nearby builds lift your land value directly.
    • Clean, clear title. Probate, liens, unpermitted additions and boundary problems all cost you money or kill deals. Sorting these early is worth real dollars.
    • Flexibility on closing. Builders juggle crews and capital. Letting them close on their timeline is often worth more than squeezing the price.
    • More than one buyer at the table. One offer is a price. Several offers is a market.

    Drags it down

    • Cosmetic money spent on a house that is coming down. New counters do not move land value. I have talked sellers out of this more than once.
    • Sitting on market too long. Days on market reads as a problem even when the house is fine, and every buyer type starts subtracting.
    • Only talking to whoever mailed you a postcard. That is one buyer's opinion, and they chose you precisely because you had no comparison.
    • Surprises found late. An issue discovered in inspection costs far more than the same issue disclosed up front, because now it is leverage.

    Going It Alone

    Why Selling It Yourself Usually Falls Short

    You are allowed to sell it yourself, and on paper it looks like the obvious way to keep more. In this particular niche it usually does not work out that way, for a reason that has nothing to do with your ability.

    Most builders will not buy directly from a homeowner. It is not personal. A builder needs clean title work, a closing that happens when they were told it would happen, and someone on the other side who understands their financing and their build schedule. They have agents they already trust for that, and they would rather pay a commission than take on an unpredictable transaction.

    So when you go it alone, the builders mostly do not show up. The people who do show up are wholesalers, who watch for-sale-by-owner listings closely. They will put your house under contract and then sell that contract to an actual buyer, and the spread they collect comes out of your side. You saved a commission and paid it to someone else, quietly.

    The other half is simply not knowing your own number. If you have never seen what the lot two streets over traded for, any offer sounds reasonable. That is the whole business model of the postcard.

    Builders Buy From People They Already Know

    I sell most of BCL Homes' South Tampa builds. That is an ongoing relationship, and it is the reason this page exists.

    Because I sell the finished homes, I know what a new build actually closes for on these streets. That is the exact number a builder starts from when they work backward to what they can pay for your lot.     It also means I can put your property in front of several builders at once rather than one. A builder who is already deep into two projects will bid politely. A builder who needs their next lot bids like they need it. Knowing which is which, right now, is most of the job.

    The Process

    What Selling As-Is Looks Like

    1

    A real conversation

    Your address, the lot, the condition, and what you are trying to accomplish. I can usually tell you quickly whether builders will be interested, and roughly where that sits against a traditional sale.

    2

    Your number, both ways

    What the land is likely worth to a builder, and what the house might bring listed as-is to a retail buyer. Those are different numbers, and you should see both before choosing.

    3

    Competition, then closing

    If land is the answer, I take it to builders who are actively buying. If listing is the answer, we list it. Either way you pick the offer, on a timeline that suits you.

    And if the honest answer is that you should keep the house, or fix one specific thing and list it retail, I will tell you that. I would rather be the person you call in three years than talk you into something today.
